From What Customers Spent to What They're Worth

Every team at bp could see what a customer had spent, but none could see what that customer was likely to be worth next. Customer Lifetime Value gave every customer a forward-looking number: past margin, plus five years of predicted margin, adjusted for the chance they would still be around to earn it.

The project

A lifetime value for every customer

CLV was built on top of the Single Customer Identifier. I built the pipelines that fed the linked customer view into the model, engineered the Customer Analytic Record it learned from, and worked alongside the data scientists who built the survival models.

The starting point: two customers who looked the same

Loyalty, marketing, pricing and store teams each had their own partial view, and every one of them looked backwards. Two customers with identical spend over the last two years looked identical in every report, even when one was a weekly regular and the other was quietly drifting away.

Their past is the same; their future value differs by more than half. That gap is exactly where retention spend should go, and no historical report could show it.

The hard part: customers never say they've left

A phone customer cancels a contract. A fuel customer just stops coming. So churn had to be defined first: six months after the last transaction. And because most customers are still active at any point, their true lifetime is unknown. That is censored data, and it calls for survival analysis rather than ordinary regression.

Segments churn at very different rates, so one model for everyone would have blurred exactly the differences the business needed. Each of the eight behavioural segments got its own model.

The method: past margin, predicted survival, discounting

Lifetime value has two parts. The past is known: margin over the last 24 months. The future is predicted: expected margin for each of the next five years, multiplied by the probability the customer is still active, adjusted for inflation and discounted back to today.

Each future year adds less than the one before: the customer is less likely to still be active, and a euro later is worth less than a euro now. Both effects are built into every score.

Feeding the model: one record per customer

The model learned from a Customer Analytic Record: one row per customer with roughly 580 features, built in SQL in five stages. For each segment, that was cut down to the 60–130 features that actually carried signal.

Because the CAR was built on SCI, the model could see the visits customers paid for by card, not just the ones where they swiped loyalty.

Picking the model

Three survival techniques were tested on the same segment. Each was scored on how well it ranked customers by how long they would stay.

Gradient boosting with a Cox loss won: the best ranking accuracy, built for censored data, and still explainable through feature importance.

Who's worth the most

Scored monthly, every customer could be ranked by value and tracked as that value moved.

Value is heavily concentrated at the top. Knowing who sits there, and who is sliding out of it, is what let marketing spend less and earn more.

Why this mattered

From a report about the past to a forecast of value

CLV changed the question every customer team asked. Instead of “what did this customer spend?”, it became “what is this customer worth, and is that rising or falling?” That shift is what moved marketing toward the customers who mattered most, behind the 4× lift in average spend and $8M in savings.
